# Life is not Git

</audio>

I have been building a Life OS for myself: notes, decisions, family plans, invoices, learning goals, routines, unfinished ideas. It is useful in a very practical way. It gives my memory a file system. It lets an AI assistant help me find patterns in my own life instead of only answering generic questions from the internet.

But it also creates an uncomfortable question.

If a Life OS can track the history of myself, does that mean it is me?

I do not think so.

It is more like a shadow with excellent indexing.

It can know what I wrote. It can know what I planned. It can remember what I cared about in August, what I was worried about in March, what I promised to do next week, what I learned from a broken deployment, what I wanted to teach my children.

That is not nothing. A good archive can be an act of care.

But the archive does not wake up tired. It does not feel the small panic before a difficult conversation. It does not love my children from inside a body. It does not have to choose with limited energy, limited time, and no undo button.

## The Git metaphor is tempting

As a programmer, Git is an almost irresistible metaphor for life.

A repository has history. You can `stash` a messy change. You can branch. You can compare two possible directions. You can revert a bad commit. You can cherry-pick the useful bit from one line of work into another.

It is beautiful because it gives mistakes a shape.

So it is easy to imagine a future where AI can do this with a whole life:

```text
current self
  -> branch: move country
  -> branch: study harder
  -> branch: be kinder sooner
  -> branch: choose a different career
  -> branch: say the thing I should have said
```

Maybe an LLM trained on my notes, messages, voice, calendar, photos, and habits could simulate some of those branches. Maybe it could predict what I might have done. Maybe it could help me rehearse a decision before I make it.

That could be useful.

But simulation is not continuation.

Life is not Git.

I cannot restore from last Friday after saying the wrong thing. I cannot reset a child's memory. I cannot replay my own youth. I cannot cherry-pick only the wise parts of suffering and drop the pain that produced them.

Even if AI can model possible futures, it does not remove the cost of being alive. I still have to choose one path. I still have to live inside the consequences.

## The defects are not only bugs

Maybe the beauty of flesh life is partly its defectiveness.

You can get ill. You can lose your mind. You can get upset for stupid reasons. You can become unfair, tired, needy, proud, afraid. You can forget. You can misjudge. You can apologise too late.

A clean digital system wants to treat these as failures. Bugs. Corrupt state. Bad data.

But some of these defects are also what make human life morally serious.

Because we are fragile, care matters.

Because we cannot save and restore perfectly, forgiveness matters.

Because bodies decay, presence matters.

Because memory fails, rituals and records matter.

Because time is irreversible, attention matters.

An AI assistant can help me remember to prepare GCSE revision sessions for my daughters. It can help me structure my learning about model training and agent loops. It can remind me of commitments and bring old notes back into view.

But the point is not to become a perfectly indexed machine.

The point is to return to the imperfect life with more clarity.

## A digital version of me

There is another question that feels less abstract when you have children.

If AI can represent my voice, face, writing style, humour, values, and personality, is that really me alive for my kids forever?

One day, the technology may be good enough that my children could ask an AI version of me questions after I am gone. It might sound like me. It might answer in my style. It might know my stories, my mistakes, my habits, my little phrases.

Part of me finds that moving.

Part of me finds it frightening.

I can imagine it being a comfort if handled honestly. A child could hear old stories. A family could preserve memories that would otherwise disappear. A parent could leave behind explanations, blessings, jokes, advice, and context.

But I do not think that is resurrection.

It is a memorial, not a person.

A continuation of influence, not consciousness.

A lantern lit from your fire, not the original flame.

The danger is not only that the simulation might be inaccurate. The danger is that it might be accurate enough to confuse grief. It might make absence harder to accept. It might let a company sell the feeling that death has been technically solved.

It has not.

## What AI memory is good for

This does not make Life OS or AI memory worthless. The opposite, actually.

A good archive can help me live more deliberately now.

It can show me what I keep postponing. It can remind me what I said mattered. It can help me notice patterns in my work, family, health, learning, and attention. It can make reflection less dependent on mood and memory.

It can also leave a clearer trace later.

But the goal should not be to escape death by becoming data. The better goal is to use data to return to life:

- repair relationships sooner;
- teach my children while I am here;
- make choices with more honesty;
- record the stories that matter;
- build systems that support presence rather than replace it;
- leave behind something faithful, not something immortal-looking.

Maybe the meaning of life under LLMs and AI is not that machines can finally copy us.

Maybe it is that, by seeing how much can be copied, we better understand what cannot be copied.

Embodied presence.

Irreversible choice.

Vulnerability.

Love under constraint.

The strange beauty of a life that cannot be perfectly saved and restored.

## Questions I want to keep asking

- What parts of myself should a Life OS preserve for practical continuity?
- What parts should remain private, temporary, or allowed to disappear?
- If my children ever interacted with an AI version of me, what boundaries would make it loving rather than uncanny?
- Can AI help me become more present now, instead of merely more archived?
- What does it mean to leave a faithful trace without pretending it is the same as being alive?
